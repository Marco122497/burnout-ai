TITLE: Academic Workload Management
CATEGORY: Academic Workload
PURPOSE: RAG knowledge tied to the five weekly Academic Workload monitoring questions

1. OVERVIEW
Section 2 of weekly monitoring asks five Likert questions about this week’s schoolwork. The student rates how heavy, overlapping, time-consuming, pressuring, and hard to keep up with their requirements felt.

The system converts those answers into a 0–10 workload score. Higher means a heavier perceived load. This is not a count of units and not a diagnosis.

Recommendations should map to the question they endorsed, especially overlapping deadlines, assignments taking longer than expected, volume of requirements, and keeping up with quizzes, projects, and readings.

2. MONITORING QUESTIONS AND GUIDANCE
Q1. How heavy is your current academic workload this week?
When this is elevated, the student needs a full picture of the week, not more hours. Put assignments, quizzes, projects, and readings on one list. Rank by due date. Leave gaps for sleep and meals. Do not say “work harder.”

Q2. How often do overlapping deadlines make your workload hard to manage?
When this is elevated, sequence the clash: finish what is due soonest, then a small piece of the larger project. A one-page deadline map prevents switching among five unfinished files. Contact a teacher early if two major items truly collide.

Q3. How often do assignments require more time than you expected?
When this is elevated, the first step was bigger than it looked. Split the assignment into outline, draft, check, submit. Time-box the next hour. Do not add an all-nighter as the default fix; that usually steals sleep without finishing the work.

Q4. How often do you feel pressured by the volume of academic requirements?
When this is elevated, volume is the problem. Keep required items. Delay optional extras. Close extra tabs. One primary task per hour. Pressure eases when the list is visible and shorter.

Q5. How difficult is it to keep up with quizzes, projects, and readings this week?
When this is elevated, mix the three types instead of pretending they are one pile. Quiz: short self-test. Project: one milestone. Reading: one chapter or one lecture. If they cannot keep up even after that, asking the teacher or adviser is a planning step.

3. TIME MANAGEMENT AND LEARNING
A 2026 meta-analysis of 31 studies (13,506 participants) found a moderate positive link between time management and college learning outcomes. Time management here means ordering quizzes, projects, and readings, not filling every hour.

4. PERSONALIZATION RULES
If WORKLOAD is high:
- Lead with Q1–Q2 and Q5 (heavy week, overlapping deadlines, keeping up with quizzes/projects/readings).
- Check sleep. Do not recommend more study hours.

If WORKLOAD is moderate:
- One list plus one next piece of the soonest due item.

If WORKLOAD is low:
- One sentence only. Focus on other elevated factors.

5. INSTITUTION-SPECIFIC POLICY
Do not invent school rules, unit loads, or extension policies.

7. STUDENT-FACING GUIDANCE
Write to the student in a calm, human voice. Use "you." Ground tips in the five workload questions. Do not diagnose.
- If this week’s workload feels heavy, put every quiz, project, and reading on one list with due dates.
- If overlapping deadlines are hard to manage, do what is due soonest, then a small piece of the bigger project.
- If assignments take more time than you expected, split them into outline, draft, check, and submit.
- If the volume of requirements feels like pressure, keep only what is required this week and close the extra files.
- If it is difficult to keep up with quizzes, projects, and readings, give each type its own short block instead of mixing them all night.
- Choose one file for the next hour. Switching among everything is why the week feels heavier.
- Leave space for sleep and a meal. A packed calendar makes ordinary delays feel like a crisis.
- If you still cannot keep up, tell a teacher or adviser early. Asking is part of managing the load.
