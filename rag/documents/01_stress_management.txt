TITLE: Psychological Stress Guidance for College Students
CATEGORY: Stress
PURPOSE: RAG knowledge for PSS-10 as a psychological measure of perceived stress, not a workload or sleep tip sheet

1. OVERVIEW
Section 1 of weekly monitoring is the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). It is a psychological instrument. Students rate how often they felt or thought a certain way. The form uses Never, Almost Never, Sometimes, Fairly Often, Very Often.

Stress in this system is the student's inner response: how they appraise school demands, how much control they feel, how they cope, and how tense the week feels in mind and body. It is not the same as Academic Workload, Study Time, or Sleep. Those are outer conditions. Stress is psychological.

The system treats the PSS total as one MFBI factor, not as a diagnosis of anxiety, depression, or burnout. Higher scores mean the student reported more perceived stress. Items 4, 5, 7, and 8 are reverse-scored: feeling confident, that things were going their way, able to control irritations, or on top of things lowers the stress score.

Score bands used by the system: 0–13 Low · 14–26 Moderate · 27–40 High.

Recommendations should answer the PSS themes they rated — control, worry, confidence, and how heavy the week feels — not generic “manage stress” slogans and not a second copy of the workload list.

2. PSYCHOLOGICAL BASIS
Perceived stress (Cohen): stress is how overloaded, unpredictable, and uncontrollable life feels, not an objective count of assignments.

Appraisal (Lazarus and Folkman): a week feels stressful when demands look bigger than the student's resources. Primary appraisal is “does this matter / does this feel like a threat.” Secondary appraisal is “can I do anything useful.” Guidance should help them name the demand, then name one resource they still have (time today, a teacher, a smaller next step).

Coping: problem-focused coping is a small action on the demand (one file, one message, one hour). Emotion-focused coping is settling the body and the looping thoughts first (a pause, a breath, a walk, writing the worry down). Both are psychological skills. Do not tell them to “just calm down” or to “just work harder.”

Self-efficacy (Bandura): confidence grows from a finished piece of work, not from pep talk. Reverse PSS items about handling problems and being on top of things point here.

Safety: this is school well-being guidance. Do not diagnose. Do not recommend medicine. Do not claim to know their mental-health status.

3. MONITORING QUESTIONS AND GUIDANCE
Q1. In the last month, how often have you been upset because of something that happened unexpectedly?
Psychological theme: surprise appraisal. The nervous system treats an unexpected quiz, deadline change, or group delay as a threat. Help them name the event and the feeling, then choose one next response. A short pause before reacting is emotion-focused coping. Do not tell them they should never feel upset.

Q2. In the last month, how often have you felt that you were unable to control the important things in your life?
Psychological theme: low perceived control. Shrink the week to what they can still choose: one deadline, one class, one message. Write the rest down so rumination is not doing the planning. Secondary appraisal: “what is mine to do today.”

Q3. In the last month, how often have you felt nervous and stressed?
Psychological theme: arousal — tense body, restless thoughts. Offer a body reset (two slow breaths, a meal, a short walk) then one under-one-hour coping step. Do not diagnose a panic disorder or anxiety disorder.

Q4. In the last month, how often have you felt confident about your ability to handle your personal problems? (reverse)
Psychological theme: low self-efficacy. A low rating raises stress. Suggest finishing one small required piece so they have proof they can handle something. Do not say “just be more confident.”

Q5. In the last month, how often have you felt that things were going your way? (reverse)
Psychological theme: the week feels stacked against them. Help them pick the soonest due item so one outcome can go their way today. That is a mastery experience, not positive thinking.

Q6. In the last month, how often have you found that you could not cope with all the things that you had to do?
Psychological theme: coping resources feel exhausted. They do not need more hours. They need a shorter demand list: keep required work, delay optional extras, break “all the things” into named steps. That restores a sense of coping.

Q7. In the last month, how often have you been able to control irritations in your life? (reverse)
Psychological theme: friction and irritability spilling across tasks. Suggest a stop-time for schoolwork and a real break between classes so one irritation is not carried into the next hour.

Q8. In the last month, how often have you felt that you were on top of things? (reverse)
Psychological theme: feeling behind, low control. A one-page list of this week’s due dates, then one file for the next hour, is a practical way to feel more on top of things.

Q9. In the last month, how often have you been angered because of things that happened that were outside of your control?
Psychological theme: anger at uncontrollability. Acknowledge the feeling without diagnosing. Point them to what they can still do (clarify a requirement, send one group message, ask a teacher) and what they can park. Do not argue them out of the feeling.

Q10. In the last month, how often have you felt difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them?
Psychological theme: overwhelm — demands look unsurmountable. Write every due item down, circle the top one, and do a piece that can be finished in under an hour. If the pile still feels impossible, talking with a teacher, adviser, or the Guidance Office is appropriate psychological and academic support.

4. PERSONALIZATION RULES
If STRESS is high:
- Prioritize psychological tips that match Q3, Q6, and Q10 (nervous/stressed, cannot cope, difficulties piling up).
- Give two stress tips: one that settles thoughts or the body, and one small coping action.
- Check sleep and workload next, but do not replace stress advice with only a to-do list.
- Do not auto-refer to counseling unless High or Severe overall risk, or the student asks.

If STRESS is moderate:
- One or two tips from Q1–Q3 or Q8 (unexpected events, control, nervousness, not on top of things).
- Encourage completing next week’s monitoring.

If STRESS is low:
- One sentence only. Do not imply they have no school difficulty.

5. SAFETY AND LIMITATIONS
Do not diagnose anxiety, depression, burnout, or any disorder. Do not replace a counselor, psychologist, or physician. If the student describes a safety concern, direct them to verified human help. Do not invent contacts.

7. STUDENT-FACING GUIDANCE
Write like a caring adviser. Use "you." Use simple, basic words. Short sentences. Do not diagnose.
Do not start every tip with "If you." Do not turn stress advice into only a homework list.
Do not use hard words such as overwhelmed, appraisal, coping resources, or self-efficacy.

HOW THIS MAY FEEL
- Stress is the worry in your thoughts and body, not just how many homework items you have.
- A surprise quiz, a changed due date, or a group delay can leave you upset even when you are trying.
- Important things may feel out of your hands, as if the week is running you.
- You may feel tense: tight chest, restless thoughts, a sense that you cannot catch up.
- When you do not feel sure of yourself, even small tasks look bigger than they are.
- Problems can feel piled so high that starting anywhere seems pointless.

THINGS YOU CAN TRY THIS WEEK
- Name the one worry sitting heaviest, so it is not going round and round in your head.
- Pick one thing you can still do today: one deadline, one class, or one message. Write the rest on paper.
- Take two slow breaths, eat or take a short walk, then give one task one honest hour. Calm down a little before you try to plan the whole week.
- Finish one small required piece today so you have proof you can handle something.
- Pick the item due first so one thing can go your way before you add more.
- Drop extra work you do not have to do. Break the rest into small steps you can finish in under an hour.
- Set a stop time for schoolwork and take a real break between classes so small annoyances do not pile into the next task.
- Write due dates on one page and open only one file for the next hour. That is enough to feel a little more on top of things.
- If the pile still feels too big, tell a teacher you trust, your adviser, or someone in Guidance. You do not have to sort a hard week alone.
