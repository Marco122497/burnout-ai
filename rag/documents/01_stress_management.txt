TITLE: Stress Management for College Students
CATEGORY: Stress
PURPOSE: RAG knowledge for the Academic Burnout Detection and Early Warning System

1. OVERVIEW
Academic stress can arise when students perceive that academic demands exceed the time, resources, or coping capacity available to them. Stress can be associated with academic difficulties and reduced well-being. The system should treat stress as one contributing factor in the user's MFBI rather than as a diagnosis.

Self-guided stress-management interventions have shown small average improvements in stress-related outcomes among college students. Evidence supports using stress-management resources as one component of a broader support system rather than presenting them as a guaranteed treatment.

2. COMMON ACADEMIC STRESSORS
Potential academic stressors include:
- Multiple assignments with overlapping deadlines
- Examinations and major projects
- High perceived academic workload
- Difficulty organizing tasks
- Lack of recovery time
- Extended study periods
- Uncertainty about academic requirements
- Difficulty balancing school with other responsibilities

The system should not assume that any single stressor is the cause of a student's result. Recommendations should be based on the student's measured factors and verified information.

3. PRACTICAL STRESS-MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES
A. Identify the main source of pressure.
Ask the student to identify which tasks, deadlines, or situations are currently creating the greatest academic pressure.

B. Break large tasks into smaller steps.
A large project can be divided into planning, research, drafting, revision, and submission. Smaller steps can make the workload easier to organize.

C. Prioritize.
Encourage students to identify urgent and important tasks first. Avoid recommending that students attempt all tasks simultaneously.

D. Schedule recovery.
Study schedules should include reasonable breaks and time for sleep, meals, and recovery.

E. Use relaxation or mindfulness activities when appropriate.
Simple breathing, mindfulness, or relaxation activities may be suggested as optional strategies. The system should not claim that these techniques work for everyone.

F. Seek social and academic support.
Students can consider talking with a trusted person, instructor, adviser, or guidance professional when academic stress becomes difficult to manage.

4. PERSONALIZATION RULES
If STRESS is high:
- Prioritize stress-management guidance.
- Look for other elevated MFBI factors before producing recommendations.
- Do not automatically recommend counseling unless the student's situation indicates that human support may be useful.
- Avoid overwhelming the student with a long list of interventions.

If STRESS is moderate:
- Provide preventive strategies such as planning, breaks, and coping techniques.
- Encourage monitoring in subsequent weekly assessments.

If STRESS is low:
- Provide maintenance-oriented advice only when appropriate.
- Do not imply that low stress means the student has no academic difficulty.

5. SAFETY AND LIMITATIONS
The RAG/LLM component must not diagnose anxiety, depression, burnout, or any other mental-health disorder. It must not claim to replace a guidance counselor, psychologist, physician, or other qualified professional.

If a student communicates severe distress, inability to function, or a safety concern, the system should direct the student to verified human support and emergency resources. It should not attempt to manage an emergency through generated advice.

6. SOURCE EVIDENCE
Amanvermez et al. (2022), Effects of self-guided stress management interventions in college students: A systematic review and meta-analysis. The review included 26 studies involving 4,468 students in its general college-student analysis and found small statistically significant effects for self-guided stress-management interventions.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35242591/

Worsley, Pennington, & Corcoran (2022), Supporting mental health and wellbeing of university and college students: A systematic review of review-level evidence of interventions. The review identified intervention types including mindfulness, psychological interventions, psychoeducation, relaxation, and stress-management/reduction approaches.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35905058/

Hathaisaard et al. (2022), Mindfulness-based interventions reducing and preventing stress and burnout in medical students: A systematic review and meta-analysis.
Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34995839/

7. STUDENT-FACING GUIDANCE
Write to the student in a calm, human voice. Use "you." Do not diagnose.
- If school feels tense, pause and name the one deadline or class that is weighing on you most this week.
- Break that work into a small next step you can finish in under an hour.
- Give yourself short breaks, a real meal, and a chance to rest. You do not have to solve everything tonight.
- If the pressure still feels too heavy, talk with a friend, a teacher you trust, or the Guidance Office.
