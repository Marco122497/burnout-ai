TITLE: Sleep and Study-Rest Balance
CATEGORY: Sleep
PURPOSE: RAG knowledge tied to the four weekly Sleep Hours monitoring questions

1. OVERVIEW
Section 4 of weekly monitoring is Sleep Hours. Four items cover: hours slept per night, how consistent the sleep schedule was, how rested they felt upon waking, and how often lack of sleep affected academics this week.

The system stores a sleep-risk score (higher means poorer rest). The CDC states most adults need at least 7 hours of sleep. This is a wellness indicator, not a medical diagnosis of insomnia or any disorder.

In the form, fewer hours, an inconsistent schedule, not feeling rested, and sleep affecting school all raise sleep-related risk. Recommendations should answer those four questions, not a generic “sleep more.”

2. MONITORING QUESTIONS AND GUIDANCE
Q1. On average, how many hours did you sleep per night this week?
Buckets used in the item: less than 5 hours, 5–6, 6–7, 7–8, more than 8. When hours are short, the first tip is to protect about 7 hours and a regular bedtime, even if one assignment is unfinished. Do not recommend an all-nighter to catch up. Tired studying usually costs more than it gives back.

Q2. How consistent was your sleep schedule this week?
When the schedule is inconsistent, keep a steadier sleep and wake time, including after a short night. A huge late sleep-in can make the next night worse. Move one study block earlier so bedtime can stay closer to normal.

Q3. How rested did you feel upon waking?
When they did not feel rested, look at late caffeine, working in bed, and screens near sleep. Keep the bed for rest. Set a stop time for schoolwork. A short wind-down beats another chapter after midnight.

Q4. How often did lack of sleep affect your academics this week?
When lack of sleep hurt classwork, treat rest as part of studying. Shorten the latest study session. Use a focused block earlier in the day. If sleep keeps harming school after they try this, a teacher, adviser, or appropriate health professional (not invented medicines) may be needed.

3. STUDY AND SLEEP BALANCE
Never recommend sacrificing sleep as a normal way to finish workload or add study hours. If sleep risk is High, it is the priority: mention it first and give more than one sleep tip.

4. PERSONALIZATION RULES
If SLEEP is high (higher risk / poorer rest):
- Lead with Q1 and Q2 (hours and schedule), then Q3–Q4 (not rested, academics affected).
- Adjust the schedule. Do not only say “sleep more.”

If SLEEP is moderate:
- One or two tips: hours or consistency.

If SLEEP is low (rest looks more adequate):
- One sentence only. Focus on other elevated factors.

5. MEDICAL LIMITATIONS
Do not diagnose insomnia, sleep apnea, depression, or anxiety. Do not recommend sleeping pills, supplements, or other medical treatments.

7. STUDENT-FACING GUIDANCE
Write like a caring adviser. Use "you." Use simple, basic words. Short sentences. Do not diagnose. Do not start every tip with "If you."

HOW THIS MAY FEEL
- Short nights can leave you coming to class already tired, even when you are trying.
- Sleep and wake times that keep changing make mornings feel rushed and evenings feel too long.
- Waking still tired often means the day starts before your body is ready.
- When lack of sleep hurts classwork, studying later usually makes the next day worse.

THINGS YOU CAN TRY THIS WEEK
- Try to sleep about 7 hours and keep a bedtime you can keep, even if one homework is not done.
- Keep sleep and wake time closer to the same each day, even after a short night. Sleeping very late can make the next night worse.
- Skip late coffee or energy drinks, dim the phone, and keep your bed for sleep, not homework.
- Move one study block earlier and stop working in time to slow down. Tired studying usually takes longer and sticks less.
- Write tomorrow's first small task on paper, then close the laptop so your mind can stop going round and round.
- Rest is part of doing well in class, not a break from school.
